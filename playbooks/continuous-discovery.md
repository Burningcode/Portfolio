# Continuous Discovery as an Operating Rhythm

Continuous discovery is not “do more interviews.” It is a repeatable way for the team building the product to connect a desired outcome, recurring customer evidence, an evolving opportunity space, and small assumption tests to real product decisions.

This playbook is an original operating adaptation informed by Teresa Torres's public [Continuous Discovery guidance](https://www.producttalk.org/continuous-discovery-habits/) and [Opportunity Solution Tree practices](https://www.producttalk.org/discovering-solutions/). It does not reproduce the book, course, or proprietary materials.

## The outcome

Create a sustainable learning system in which evidence changes priorities before the team makes expensive or irreversible commitments.

The minimum loop is:

```text
Desired outcome
      ↓
Customer stories + product behavior + operational evidence
      ↓
Opportunity map
      ↓
Multiple solution responses
      ↓
Assumption tests
      ↓
Decision and updated opportunity map
```

## When continuous discovery is working

- Product, design, and engineering share the evidence rather than handing off conclusions.
- The team can connect current work to an outcome and a customer opportunity.
- Recent customer stories are available without launching a large research project.
- Solutions compete on assumptions and evidence, not advocacy.
- Failed tests remove or reshape work before it becomes expensive.
- Stakeholders can see what changed in the team's understanding and why.

## Set the discovery contract

```text
Outcome and metric contract:
Decision owner:
Core product trio or working group:
Target users and excluded populations:
Evidence cadence:
Current opportunity focus:
Decision review cadence:
```

A team with one outcome and a modest evidence rhythm will learn more than a team running many disconnected studies.

## Build five operating habits

### 1. Anchor on one outcome

Define the observable user behavior and business result the team is trying to create. Include population, time window, authoritative source, threshold hypothesis, and guardrails.

The outcome creates discovery boundaries. It should not dictate a solution.

### 2. Maintain customer access

Create a recruitment path that does not depend on heroic effort:

- in-product opt-in or research panel;
- customer-success or support referral with consent;
- scheduled sessions with representative design partners;
- usability intercepts; or
- operational shadowing where appropriate.

Track who is being heard and who is missing. Repeated access to the same enthusiastic segment can create false confidence.

### 3. Capture specific customer stories

Ask about a real recent event rather than a hypothetical preference.

```text
Tell me about the last time you tried to [make progress].
What triggered it?
What did you do first?
What happened next?
Where did you hesitate, switch tools, ask for help, or stop?
What was the consequence?
```

Record the trigger, behavior, context, workaround, consequence, and evidence limitation. A quote is useful; the underlying behavior is more useful.

### 4. Keep an opportunity map

An opportunity is an unmet need, pain point, desire, or progress barrier—not a feature request.

| Opportunity | Customer story/evidence | Segment/context | Outcome link | Confidence | Open question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Need or barrier] | [Source/date] | [Who/when] | [Behavior affected] | [H/M/L] | [Unknown] |

Group opportunities by causal relationship, not by UI area or org chart. Retain contradictions; do not force every interview into the current taxonomy.

### 5. Test assumptions, not entire business cases

For each candidate solution, identify assumptions across:

- **value:** the user will choose or benefit from it;
- **usability:** the user can understand and use it;
- **feasibility:** the team and system can deliver it;
- **viability:** the business and operating model can sustain it;
- **safety/trust:** failure and misuse remain within acceptable bounds.

Prioritize by importance and uncertainty. Test the assumption whose failure would invalidate the largest amount of work.

## Assumption test card

```text
Decision this test informs:
Opportunity:
Candidate solution:
Assumption:
Why it has a large blast radius:
Method and eligible population:
Primary signal and authoritative source:
Threshold / decision rule:
Guardrails:
Owner, duration, and stopping condition:
```

The test should be as small as possible, but credible enough to change the decision. A fast test that nobody trusts creates speed without learning.

## A practical weekly rhythm

The exact days are optional; the recurring decisions are not.

| Moment | Team activity | Durable output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evidence intake | Review product, support, operational, and market signals | New evidence and contradictions |
| Customer touchpoint | Observe or interview users in context | Specific stories |
| Synthesis | Update opportunities and confidence | Opportunity-map change log |
| Assumption test | Run or review the smallest credible test | Result and evidence quality |
| Decision review | Continue, modify, stop, or change focus | Decision log and next owner |

For low-volume enterprise products, “continuous” may mean a reliable biweekly rhythm plus ongoing behavioral and operational evidence. The discipline is that evidence remains connected to decisions—not that every team follows an identical calendar.

## Discovery review: 30 minutes

1. **Outcome:** Has the metric or relevant segment changed?
2. **Evidence:** What did we observe since the last review?
3. **Opportunity map:** What moved, split, combined, or lost confidence?
4. **Tests:** Which assumption became more or less likely?
5. **Decision:** What will we continue, change, stop, or investigate next?
6. **Ownership:** Who produces the next proof point, and when?

This is not a status meeting. If no evidence or decision has changed, end early.

## Evidence and decision log

| Date | Evidence | Quality/limitation | Map change | Decision affected | Owner/next proof |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Date] | [Observation/result] | [Bias/gap] | [Opportunity change] | [Continue/modify/stop] | [Owner/date] |

This log prevents the team from resetting to old assumptions when people, priorities, or agents change.

## Fictional example: team expense approvals

**Outcome:** More eligible employees submit a compliant expense correctly on the first attempt without increasing finance review time.

**Customer stories:** Employees describe saving receipts in multiple places, guessing which category applies, and learning a policy exception only after rejection.

**Opportunity:** Employees need to understand the policy consequence at the moment they classify an expense, not after submission.

**Candidate responses:** Contextual policy guidance, suggested categories with rationale, or a pre-submission review.

**Riskiest assumption:** Category uncertainty—not missing documentation or manager delay—is the dominant source of avoidable rework.

**Test:** For two high-volume categories, show policy guidance in a realistic prototype using recent anonymized scenarios. Measure correct first-choice classification and explanation comprehension. Stop or reframe if errors persist for reasons unrelated to policy interpretation.

## Adoption path

### First 30 days

- Choose one outcome and one decision owner.
- Establish a repeatable customer-access path.
- Run three story-based conversations or observations.
- Start a simple opportunity and evidence log.

### Days 31–60

- Involve product, design, and engineering in evidence review.
- Generate multiple responses to one opportunity.
- Run one assumption test with a decision rule.
- Share a short playback showing what changed.

### Days 61–90

- Connect discovery decisions to roadmap and delivery work.
- Audit segment coverage and evidence gaps.
- Track stopped or reshaped work as learning value.
- Adjust the cadence so it remains sustainable.

## Common failure modes

- **Interview theater:** conversations occur, but no decision or map changes.
- **Solution tree:** feature requests are mislabeled as opportunities.
- **Research handoff:** one function gathers evidence and others receive conclusions.
- **Metric monoculture:** behavioral data explains what happened but not the mechanism.
- **Quote-driven prioritization:** vivid anecdotes outrank population and consequence.
- **Testing trivia:** teams test easy assumptions rather than fatal ones.
- **Cadence as compliance:** weekly activity continues after it stops producing useful evidence.

## Agent-ready adaptation

Agents can prepare evidence packs, produce transcript snapshots, label claims, propose opportunity clusters, search for contradictions, draft assumption tests, and maintain traceability. Humans must approve research use, protect consent and privacy, interpret organizational context, choose the opportunity, and make product commitments. Agent synthesis should link back to the underlying source so the team can inspect it.

## Quality gate

- One desired outcome scopes discovery.
- The team has recurring access to representative users or equivalent evidence.
- Opportunities describe needs or barriers, not features.
- Every map change has a source and confidence level.
- Multiple solution mechanisms exist for the selected opportunity.
- The riskiest assumption has a credible test and decision rule.
- Results change a named product decision.
- Privacy, consent, and human accountability remain explicit.
